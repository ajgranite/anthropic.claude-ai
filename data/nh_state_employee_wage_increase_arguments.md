# Arguments Supporting Wage/COLA Increases for NH State Employees

## Executive Summary

New Hampshire state employees face a significant compensation gap compared to private sector and municipal workers, while cost of living has risen dramatically. This document compiles data supporting the case for wage increases and Cost of Living Adjustments (COLA).

---

## 1. Cost of Living Crisis in New Hampshire

### Housing Costs Have Skyrocketed
- **Housing costs rose 113%** from 2015 to 2024
- Median single-family home price reached **$569,450** in June 2025 (record high)
- This is **more than 4x the 1999 median** of ~$137,000
- Mortgage rates have **more than doubled** since 2015
- Housing costs are **26% higher than national average**
- Portsmouth median home price: **$817,000**

### Overall Cost of Living
- NH cost of living is **12-15% higher than national average**
- Monthly costs: **$2,846/month** for singles, **$6,268/month** for families of four
- Typical resident spends approximately **$65,908 annually** on goods and services
- **A typical NH family does not have enough money to cover basic living expenses** (NHPR, Oct 2025)

## 2. State Employee Pay Gap vs. Private Sector

### Significant Pay Disparity
- State employees receive **lower wages than private sector counterparts**
- Applicants can get **20% more** for the same job at municipalities or private sector
- **Example:** City of Rochester offered **$109,000/year** for an assessor while the state offered **$80,000** for the same position

### Teacher Pay Gap
- Teachers are paid **14.3% less** than comparable private sector workers
- This pay gap has **increased in the last decade**

### Current State Employee Compensation
- Average state employee salary: **$60,054**
- Median state employee salary: **$59,215**
- Total state employees in database: **76,183**

## 3. Critical Vacancy and Retention Crisis

### State Vacancy Rates
- Overall vacancy rate reached **~19%** (vs. historical average of ~11%)
- At one point, vacancy rate hit approximately **30%**
- Government employment **declined by 1,400 jobs** over the year (Aug 2025)
- Jobs remaining open for **6+ months**

### Temporary Staffing Costs Exploding
- NH Executive Council approved **tripling the temporary staffing budget** from **$3.8 million to $11.5 million** (through mid-2025)
- State facilities forced to rely increasingly on expensive temporary staff

### Healthcare/Behavioral Health Staffing Crisis
- Hospital vacancy rate: **11%**
- Registered nurse vacancy rate: **14%**
- Nursing job postings increased from 746/month (2021) to **2,259/month** (first half 2025)
- Posting-to-hiring ratio: nearly **5-to-1**

### Retention Measures Already Needed
- State has offered **$5,000 retention bonuses** just to keep current employees

## 4. Behavioral Health Workforce Economic Argument

### Current Reality
> "Right now, it is counter to an individual's economic self-interest to work in a behavioral health career in NH."

- Workforce shortages have remained at "alarming levels" for **nearly a decade**
- Individuals in clear need of behavioral health support are **not getting sufficient help**
- "All of the easy solutions have been tried and exhausted"

## 5. Demographic Time Bomb

### Aging Workforce
- **31% of NH's labor force** is 55 or older
- **9.2%** are 65 or older
- NH does **not have a younger population large enough** to fill jobs left by upcoming retirements

### Implication
Without competitive wages, the state will face even more severe staffing shortages as experienced workers retire and younger workers choose better-paying alternatives.

### Sources
- [NHFPI: NH Labor Force Growth May Not Continue](https://nhfpi.org/blog/new-hampshires-labor-force-grew-in-2024-but-growth-might-not-continue/)

## 6. Recent Contract History Shows Inadequate Increases

### Previous Increases
- 2023-2025 contract: **2% pay increase**
- Governor's 2024-2025 proposal: targeted **10% increase in SFY 2024** and **2% in SFY 2025**

### The Math Doesn't Work
- Housing up **113%** since 2015
- Cost of living **12-15% above national average**
- Single-digit percentage raises cannot keep pace with these increases

### Sources
- [SEA/SEIU Local 1984 Contract](https://seiu1984.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/2023-2025-State-SEA-Master-Contract-1.pdf)

## 7. Key Arguments Summary

| Issue | Data Point | Impact |
|-------|------------|--------|
| Housing affordability | 113% increase since 2015 | Workers cannot afford to live near jobs |
| Pay gap | 20% less than private sector/municipal | Talent chooses other employers |
| Vacancy crisis | 19% vacancy rate (vs 11% historical) | Services suffer, remaining staff burned out |
| Temporary staff costs | Budget tripled to $11.5M | More expensive than permanent raises |
| Aging workforce | 31% of labor force 55+ | Retirements will worsen crisis |
| Retention bonuses | $5,000 bonuses already needed | Band-aid solution, not sustainable |

---

## 8. Recommended Talking Points

1. **Cost Argument**: Paying temp staff 3x more than investing in permanent employee wages is fiscally irresponsible.

2. **Recruitment Argument**: A 20% pay gap with private sector/municipal jobs means the state cannot compete for talent.

3. **Service Delivery Argument**: 19% vacancy rates mean NH residents are not receiving the services they need and pay taxes for.

4. **Retention Argument**: Retention bonuses prove the problem exists; sustainable salary increases are the solution.

5. **COLA Argument**: With housing up 113% and cost of living 15% above national average, wages that don't keep pace force workers into poverty or out of state employment.

6. **Future Workforce Argument**: With 31% of the workforce nearing retirement, competitive wages are essential to attract the next generation.
